Before You Begin

You've arrived late to the parlor.

“Imagine that you enter a parlor. You come late. When you arrive, others have long preceded you, and they are engaged in a heated discussion... You listen for a while, until you decide that you have caught the tenor of the argument; then you put in your oar.”

The conversation is already underway. You do not begin it. You enter it.

To enter, your group must demonstrate that you understand rhetoric as an ethical art: quoting accurately, paraphrasing honestly, summarizing without distortion, and citing sources correctly.

Four brass locks guard the door. Each solved lock reveals one digit. The house assigns a different combination to each group session.

Lock I · The Quotation

The Dropped Quote

A student's sentence contains a quotation, but something has gone wrong:

“Rhetoric is a tool that has two sides. ‘as Hitler and other dictators have done.’ This shows that rhetoric is dangerous.”

What is the actual problem?

Think about what readers need before a quotation appears. How do they know whose words they are reading and why the quotation belongs in the sentence?

Lock II · The Paraphrase

The Copied Rewrite

Original passage:

“...we need to understand rhetoric both to communicate our own ethical messages and to be able to recognize and resist unethical messages that others attempt to use against us.”

Which rewrite is a genuine paraphrase—not simply the original with a few words exchanged?

A genuine paraphrase changes more than vocabulary. Look for a version that genuinely re-expresses the idea in a new sentence structure.

Lock III · The Summary

The Distorted Message

A student summarizes the reading this way:

“The author argues that rhetoric should never be used to persuade people because it was the tool dictators like Hitler used to gain power (Lunsford 14).”

What does this summary get wrong about Lunsford's idea?

Pay attention to the word never. Does Lunsford condemn rhetoric itself, or distinguish between ethical and unethical uses of it?

Lock IV · The Citation

The Citation Seal

Your group wants to cite a direct quotation from page 14 of Lunsford's book using MLA style.

Which in-text citation is correctly formatted?

MLA normally uses the author's last name and page number without a comma and without “p.”

RHETORIC IS AN ETHICAL ART

The conversation was already underway when you arrived. Listen first. Understand the audience and purpose. Choose language deliberately. Then contribute responsibly.

Then put in your oar.

The house notices.

Two quick wrong choices look more like guessing than discussion.

Talk through the evidence together before you try again.
